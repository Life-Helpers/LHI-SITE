=== LESSON: Why NGOs are targets ===
Summary: What attackers want from organisations like LHI, and why people are the first line of defence.
Minutes: 5

Humanitarian organisations hold things criminals want: money moving to suppliers and partners, donor relationships, and personal data about vulnerable people. Many attacks do not break in through technology; they trick a person into opening the door.

## What attackers are after
- Money: changing a supplier's bank details, or a fake "urgent payment" request from a director.
- Accounts: your email password, which opens shared drives, finance systems and contacts.
- Data: beneficiary lists, staff records and payroll, which can be sold or used to harm people.
- Disruption: ransomware that locks files until a ransom is paid.

[!info] You are the first line of defence
Most successful attacks start with a click, a reply or a shared password. A few careful habits stop the great majority of them.

## Everyone's responsibilities
- Use LHI accounts and devices for LHI work.
- Follow the ICT policy and the steps in this course.
- Report anything suspicious to the ICT Unit straight away, even if you think it is probably nothing or you already clicked.

Why: Most attacks rely on deceiving people rather than defeating technology.

=== LESSON: Passwords and two-step verification ===
Summary: Strong passphrases, never reusing them, and turning on two-step verification.
Minutes: 6

Your password protects everything your account can reach. A weak or reused password is the easiest way in.

## A strong password
- Long: at least 12 characters. A passphrase of four random words, such as "mango-bicycle-river-lantern", is long, strong and easy to remember.
- Unique: a different password for every account. If one website is breached, criminals try the same password everywhere else.
- Private: never shared with anyone, including colleagues, ICT or your supervisor. ICT will never ask for your password.
- Stored safely: in a password manager approved by ICT, not on paper stuck to your screen or in a phone note.

## Two-step verification (2SV)
Two-step verification, also called multi-factor authentication, asks for a second proof after your password, such as a code from an authenticator app or a prompt on your phone. Even if someone steals your password, they cannot get in without your phone.

[!warning] Never approve a sign-in you didn't start
If your phone asks you to approve a sign-in or gives you a code you did not request, someone has your password. Deny it, change your password and tell ICT. Never read a verification code out to anyone who calls you.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: Which is the strongest password?
- Lhi2026!
* mango-bicycle-river-lantern
- your date of birth
Why: Long passphrases of random words are much harder to crack than short complex ones.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: Someone calls from "ICT" and asks for the code just sent to your phone. You:
- Read it out, since it is ICT
* Refuse, hang up and report the call
- Send it by SMS instead
Why: Verification codes are never shared; this is a common account-takeover trick.

=== LESSON: Spotting phishing and payment scams ===
Summary: The warning signs of fake emails, messages and calls, and the rule for any change of bank details.
Minutes: 8

Phishing is a message designed to make you click a link, open an attachment, enter your password or send money. It can come by email, WhatsApp, SMS or a phone call, and it often looks like it comes from someone you trust.

## Warning signs
- Urgency or pressure: "pay today", "your account will be closed", "keep this confidential".
- A sender address that is slightly wrong, such as lhinigeria.co or a Gmail address for a director.
- Unexpected attachments or links, especially to "view a document" or "verify your account".
- Requests to buy airtime or gift cards, or to change payment details.
- Poor spelling, or a greeting that doesn't sound like the person.

## Before you click
1. Hover over a link to see where it really goes.
2. If in doubt, open the website yourself instead of using the link.
3. Check with the sender using a phone number or contact you already have, not the details in the message.

[!warning] Bank details never change by email alone
Business email compromise is one of the costliest scams: a criminal sends a convincing email, apparently from a supplier or partner, saying their bank account has changed. Any request to change bank details must be confirmed by phone with a known contact at the supplier, and approved by Finance, before any payment is made.

The same applies to an "urgent" payment request from a manager or director. Real leaders will not mind you checking.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: A supplier emails new bank details for this month's payment. What do you do?
- Update the details and pay, since the email looks genuine
- Reply to the email to confirm
* Call the supplier on a number you already have, and get Finance approval
Why: Replies go to the scammer; only an independent check confirms a real change.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: Which is a common sign of phishing?
* Pressure to act urgently and keep it confidential
- A message from a colleague about a meeting you both arranged
- A newsletter you subscribed to
Why: Scammers use urgency and secrecy to stop you from checking.

=== LESSON: Safe devices, Wi-Fi and files ===
Summary: Updates, screen locks, public Wi-Fi, USB sticks, backups and travelling with devices.
Minutes: 6

Your laptop and phone carry LHI email, files and often beneficiary data. Losing one, or letting malware onto it, puts all of that at risk.

## Device habits
- Install updates for your operating system, browser and apps as soon as they are offered.
- Lock your screen whenever you step away (Windows key + L), and set a PIN or password on your phone.
- Only install software from official sources approved by ICT.
- Keep antivirus switched on.
- Don't let family or friends use your work device.

## Wi-Fi and USB
- Avoid public Wi-Fi for finance or sensitive work; use your mobile data or a VPN approved by ICT.
- Don't plug in USB sticks you found or were given at events; they can carry malware.

## Files and backups
- Save work in LHI's shared drives, which are backed up, not only on your desktop.
- Share files with named people, not "anyone with the link", when they contain personal or financial data.

[!info] Travelling to the field
Carry only the data you need, keep devices with you rather than in vehicles, and report a lost or stolen device to ICT immediately so it can be locked or wiped.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: You find a USB stick in the car park labelled "Salaries 2026". You:
- Plug it in to find the owner
* Hand it to ICT without plugging it in
- Take it home
Why: Unknown USB sticks are a classic way to spread malware.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: Why install updates promptly?
* They fix security weaknesses that attackers already know about
- They make the device slower
- They are only for new features
Why: Updates close known security holes.

=== LESSON: When something goes wrong ===
Summary: Recognising an incident and the exact steps to take in the first minutes.
Minutes: 5

Incidents happen even to careful people. What matters most is reporting quickly. The first hour often decides whether an incident is a small problem or a serious loss.

## Signs of an incident
- You clicked a suspicious link or entered your password on a strange page.
- Your account sends messages you didn't write, or you are locked out.
- Files are renamed, encrypted or disappear; a message demands payment.
- A device is lost or stolen.
- Personal or financial data was sent to the wrong person.

## What to do
1. Stop: don't keep clicking or try to fix it alone.
2. Disconnect: turn off Wi-Fi or unplug the network cable if you suspect malware or ransomware.
3. Report: call or message the ICT Unit immediately, then tell your supervisor.
4. Change your password from a different, safe device if your account may be compromised.
5. Write down what happened and when, while you remember.

[!success] No blame for reporting
LHI will never punish anyone for reporting a mistake promptly. Hiding an incident is what causes the real damage.

If personal data may have been exposed, the data protection focal point must also be told, because the law requires serious breaches to be reported within a short deadline.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: Your screen shows files have been encrypted and a payment is demanded. Your first steps are:
- Pay the ransom quickly
* Disconnect from the network and report to ICT immediately
- Restart and keep working
Why: Disconnecting limits the spread; ICT must lead the response.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: Your work phone is stolen in the market. When do you tell ICT?
* Immediately, so the device and accounts can be locked
- After you have bought a new phone
- Only if it had beneficiary data
Why: Quick reporting lets ICT protect every account on the phone.
