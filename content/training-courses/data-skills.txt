=== LESSON: Types of data and what good data looks like ===
Summary: Numbers and words, indicators and records, and the signs of data you can rely on.
Minutes: 6

Data is any information we record to understand our work. At LHI it includes beneficiary registers, survey answers, distribution lists, stock records, attendance sheets, finance transactions and feedback logs.

## Two kinds of data
- Quantitative data is numbers you can count or measure: how many people attended, how many kilograms were delivered, what percentage of households have soap.
- Qualitative data is words and observations: what people said in a focus group, why women stopped attending a session, a complaint in someone's own words.

Both are needed. Numbers show how much; words explain why.

## Signs of good data
- Complete: no missing rows or blank required fields.
- Accurate: values are correct and match the source document.
- Consistent: the same thing is written the same way everywhere (one spelling for each LGA).
- Timely: it is entered soon after collection, while questions can still be checked.
- Traceable: every figure can be followed back to a form, register or record.

[!info] Rubbish in, rubbish out
No amount of analysis can fix data that was recorded wrongly. Most data problems are prevented by careful collection and entry, not by clever formulas later.

=== LESSON: Setting up a clean spreadsheet ===
Summary: Simple rules that make data easy to count, filter and share.
Minutes: 7

Most of our data lives in Excel or Google Sheets. A few rules make the difference between a sheet you can analyse in minutes and one that takes days to fix.

## The rules of a tidy sheet
1. One table per sheet, starting in the top-left cell.
2. One header row, with short, clear column names and no merged cells.
3. One row for each record (one person, one household, one distribution).
4. One piece of information per column: put first name and surname, or state and LGA, in separate columns.
5. Use dates in one format, such as 2026-11-18, and store numbers as numbers, not text.
6. Use drop-down lists (data validation) for fixed choices like sex, LGA or disability status.
7. Never use colour alone to carry meaning; add a column instead.
8. Give each record a unique ID so it can be found and never counted twice.

[!warning] Keep the raw data untouched
Save the original download or entry sheet as it is, and do your cleaning and analysis in a copy or a separate sheet. If something goes wrong you can always go back.

Name files so others can find them, for example 2026-11_Sokoto_PDM_Raw.xlsx, and keep them in the shared drive folder for the project rather than on a personal laptop.

=== LESSON: Cleaning data ===
Summary: Finding and fixing duplicates, blanks, typos and impossible values, and recording what you changed.
Minutes: 8

Cleaning means finding and fixing errors before analysis. Do it every time new data arrives, not just at the end of the project.

## What to look for
- Duplicates: the same person or household entered twice. Check the unique ID, and also name, phone number and community together.
- Blanks: required fields left empty.
- Typos and inconsistent spellings: use filters to list every value in a column and spot odd ones.
- Impossible values: an age of 250, a pregnant man, a distribution date in the future, a household of 0 people.
- Outliers: values that are possible but unusual, such as one household receiving ten kits. Check these against the source before changing anything.

## Useful spreadsheet tools
- Filter and sort to see all values in a column.
- Remove duplicates, after checking which copy is correct.
- TRIM and PROPER to tidy extra spaces and capital letters.
- COUNTIF to find values that appear more than once.
- Conditional formatting to highlight blanks and values outside a range.

[!warning] Never guess
If you cannot confirm the correct value from the form, register or the person who collected it, mark it as missing. A blank is honest; a guess is a false record.

Keep a short cleaning log: what you found, what you changed, why, who did it and when. It protects you in an audit and lets others trust your figures.

=== LESSON: Simple analysis and presenting results ===
Summary: Counts, percentages, disaggregation and charts that tell the truth.
Minutes: 8

Most programme questions are answered with simple analysis: how many, what percentage, and how it differs between groups.

## Everyday analysis
- Counts and sums: how many people were reached (COUNTIFS, SUMIFS).
- Percentages: the share who meet a standard, always with the number it is a percentage of ("62% of 340 households").
- Averages: use the median when a few extreme values could mislead.
- Pivot tables: the fastest way to summarise by LGA, sex, age group or month.
- Change over time: compare with the baseline and the previous period.

## Disaggregate to see who is left out
Totals can hide gaps. Break results down by sex, age group, disability and location. If 80% of all households received kits but only 45% of households headed by a person with a disability did, the total hides a problem we must act on.

## Charts that tell the truth
- Bar charts for comparing groups, line charts for change over time.
- Start the value axis at zero for bar charts.
- Give every chart a clear title, labelled axes, the period and the source.
- Avoid 3D and pie charts with many slices; they are hard to read.

[!info] Avoid double counting
One person who attends three sessions is one person reached but three attendances. Be clear which you are reporting, and use unique IDs to count people.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: Which is the clearest way to report a percentage?
- "62%"
* "62% of 340 households (211)"
- "Most households"
Why: Giving the base number lets readers judge the result.

--- PRACTICE QUESTION ---
Q: A woman attended three trainings. How many people were reached?
- Three
* One
- Zero
Why: People reached counts individuals once; attendances count each visit.

=== LESSON: Protecting personal data ===
Summary: What personal data is, the law that protects it, and the daily habits that keep it safe.
Minutes: 7

Personal data is any information that identifies a living person: a name, phone number, photo, address, ID number or fingerprint. Some is sensitive, such as health status, disability, religion, ethnicity or experience of violence. Leaking it can put people in real danger.

In Nigeria, personal data is protected by the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, overseen by the Nigeria Data Protection Commission. Donors and LHI's own policies add further rules.

## Principles to follow
- Collect only what the project needs (data minimisation).
- Tell people why you collect it and get their consent.
- Use it only for the purpose you explained.
- Keep it accurate and up to date.
- Keep it only as long as needed, then delete or destroy it securely.
- Keep it secure and share it only with people who need it.

## Daily habits
- Store data on LHI's approved systems, not on personal phones, USB sticks or personal email.
- Password-protect files with personal data and share them through access-controlled links, not attachments to large groups.
- Remove names and phone numbers before sharing data for analysis or reports (anonymise or use IDs).
- Lock away paper forms and shred them when no longer needed.
- Never post photos or stories of beneficiaries on social media without their informed consent and approval through communications.

[!warning] Report a data breach immediately
If personal data is lost, sent to the wrong person or accessed without permission, report it to your supervisor and the data protection focal point at once. Quick action limits the harm, and serious breaches must be reported to the authorities within a short deadline.
